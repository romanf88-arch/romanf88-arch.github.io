Read the Market

Due Wednesday, September 2, 11:59 p.m. ·Started in Wednesday's session ·About 45 minutes

Why we are doing this

This activity is meant to help you build labor market literacy. Instead of guessing what employers want, you will read real job postings and look for evidence. This will help you move from a general career interest to evidence-based professional positioning: being able to explain what kind of work you are preparing for, what employers are asking for, and what evidence you can build to show that you are ready.

This also connects to capstone planning. Your capstone should not just be something interesting; it should help you create evidence of skills, tools, or experience that matter for the kind of work you may want after graduation.

You will also practice metacognition by explaining what you learned, checking your understanding, and talking it through with a partner.

Finally, this activity prepares you for authentic professional communication. Next week, you will draft a short professional introduction that could help someone understand what opportunities fit you and why.

The big idea

This week, we are connecting three things:

  1. What the market is asking for
  2. What kind of work holds your attention
  3. What evidence you want to build next

Today's focus is the first one: what the market is asking for.

What you will submit

At the end of this activity, you will submit a short summary in Canvas. Your summary should be about 150 words and should include:

  1. The kind of work you searched for.
  2. How many job postings you read.
  3. One opportunity you found.
  4. One challenge you found.
  5. Quoted evidence from the postings.
  6. One thing you could do differently this semester because of what you learned.
  7. Notes from your partner conversation.
Learning outcome

By the end of this activity, you should be able to say:

I found two things about the market that I did not know before: one opportunity and one challenge. For each one, I can explain what I saw in the job postings and what I could do differently because of it.

In other words, you are not just reporting what job postings say. You are using the postings to make a decision about your own next steps.

As you read, look for repeated patterns. Do not only look at the job title. Pay attention to:

  • preferred skills

  • teamwork expectations

6

Notice what might change

Job markets change. Choose one thing you expect may change in this work area. This does not need to be a perfect prediction. The goal is to practice watching the market over time.

Write three things: what you expect to change, what might drive that change, and what signal you would watch.

Examples of drivers

A driver is something that may cause change: AI tools, cybersecurity threats, new regulations, cloud adoption, automation, budget cuts, demand for data, remote work policies, changes in hiring practices.

Examples of signals

A signal is evidence you could watch later: more postings requiring a specific tool, fewer entry-level postings, more postings asking for AI experience, more postings asking for security knowledge, more postings requiring a portfolio, changes in salary ranges, changes in internship or new-graduate programs.

Examples

These examples show the kind of thinking this activity is asking for. They are models, not student submissions.

Example 1 · Entry-level software engineering

I searched for entry-level software engineering roles and read six postings.

OpportunityThree of the six postings mentioned side projects, portfolio work, or shipped products. One posting asked for a "portfolio of side projects," and another asked for "experience building and deploying applications." I had assumed that coursework alone would be the main evidence for a new graduate. These postings show that employers may value public, finished work even when someone does not have much job experience yet. Because of this, I could shape my capstone into something deployed, documented, and easy to show.

ChallengeFour of the six postings mentioned cloud tools, deployment, or production systems. One posting asked for "experience with AWS or similar cloud platforms," and another mentioned "building services in a production environment." I have written code for classes, but I have not always deployed it or maintained it like a real product. Because of this, I could use my capstone to practice deployment, documentation, testing, and version control.

Example 2 · Cybersecurity

I searched for new-graduate cybersecurity roles and read five postings.

OpportunityTwo postings listed bug bounty, CTF, or live hacking experience as preferred qualifications. One posting said "CTF experience preferred," and another said "bug bounty or responsible disclosure experience is a plus." I did not realize that some evidence for cybersecurity roles could come from activities outside internships. Because of this, I could join a CTF event this semester and document what I learn in a public write-up or portfolio.

ChallengeThree postings expected programming experience in languages I have not used much. One posting asked for "Python, Go, or Rust," and another asked for "ability to read and review code." I thought cybersecurity would mostly focus on tools and concepts, but these postings show that code review and programming matter too. Because of this, I could choose one language, such as Python or Go, and use it in my capstone or security practice.

Example 3 · Data analyst

I searched for entry-level data analyst roles and read six postings.

OpportunityFour postings mentioned dashboards, visualization, or communicating results to non-technical audiences. One posting asked for "clear dashboards for business stakeholders," and another asked for "ability to explain findings to non-technical teams." I had thought data analyst roles were mostly about technical analysis. These postings show that communication is part of the job. Because of this, I could make sure my capstone includes a dashboard and a short explanation written for a real audience.

ChallengeFive postings mentioned SQL, and three mentioned Tableau, Power BI, or a similar visualization tool. One posting said "strong SQL required," and another asked for "experience with Tableau or Power BI." I have used data in classes, but I am not sure I can show strong evidence with these specific tools yet. Because of this, I could build a small project this semester that uses SQL queries and a dashboard, then include screenshots and a short explanation in my portfolio.
